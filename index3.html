Reading Hobbes's Leviathan

Posted Thursday 05/01/2025 in Reading

permalink

We read (parts of) Thomas Hobbes's Leviathan (1651) in April and early May 2025. It is one of the founding texts of modern political philosophy, and reading it is about as enjoyable as drinking five cement milkshakes in a row.

Hobbes's arguments have all the subtlety of a boiling pot of water thrown in your face. And they're written in 17th-century English prose that seems to simultaneously look down its nose at you, treats you like you have the intelligence of a toddler, and is trapped in an age when people were much more close-minded and ignorant. The organization is not much better - massive 4-part books, with 47 chapters, and headers that are long run-on sentences - so the whole thing is a frustrating exercise. One might even use the phrase "interminable biblical exegesis," if one were reaching for ten dollar words.

Full notes on the wiki: Leviathan.

The Argument in One Paragraph

Hobbes says: if there were no government, life would be a war of everyone against everyone else, and would be "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." To escape this, rational individuals agree (hypothetically, via a "social contract") to give up nearly all their natural rights and submit to an absolute sovereign - a person or an assembly - whose job is to keep the peace by force.

Equally important is Hobbes' proposition that any sovereign, no matter how bad, is preferable to no sovereign at all, because anarchy is worse than tyranny.

That is the whole book. Everything else is Hobbes methodically building the argument up from first principles, then defending it against every objection he can think of - including all the biblical objections that mattered in 1651 England.

Why This Book Was Written When It Was

Hobbes was writing during and immediately after the English Civil War (1642-1651). Charles I had been executed. The country had spent almost a decade in a chaotic three-way war between royalists, parliamentarians, and various religious factions. Hobbes's family had fled to France. When he wrote Leviathan, "war of all against all" was not a thought experiment - it was recent memory.

This context matters because modern readers instinctively resist Hobbes's absolutism. Absolute sovereignty sounds like tyranny. But Hobbes had watched what happens when there is no sovereign, and he thought tyranny was clearly the lesser evil.

The book is aimed at people who lived through the same collapse he did. Reading it in a functioning democracy in 2025 is a very different experience than reading it in 1651.

The Four Parts

Leviathan has four parts, roughly:

Part I: Of Man (16 chapters). Hobbes builds up an account of human nature from mechanistic first principles - all thought comes from sensory experience, all action is motion, all motivation is appetite or aversion. He argues that humans are roughly equal in capability, driven by "a perpetual and restless desire of power after power, that ceaseth only in death," and so in the absence of a common power, they will inevitably fight each other for competition, safety, and glory.

Personal opinion: this is dreadfully boring - it has all the action-packed excitement of (insert the most boring Old Testament passage imaginable here) - and is cringe-inducing.

Part II: Of Commonwealth (15 chapters). Given the argument in Part I, Hobbes derives the necessity of an absolute sovereign, lays out the sovereign's rights (which are essentially unlimited), and describes the three types of commonwealth (monarchy, aristocracy, democracy). He argues monarchy is best because the monarch's private interest is most closely aligned with the public interest.

Personal opinion: the only portion of this book actually worth anything.

Part III: Of a Christian Commonwealth (11 chapters). Hobbes argues that religious authority must be subordinate to civil authority in a Christian society. This part is a lot of biblical interpretation, most of which is only interesting if you care about the 17th-century debate over church-state relations.

Personal opinion: worthless, skip.

Part IV: Of the Kingdom of Darkness (4 chapters). A polemic against the Catholic Church and Aristotelian philosophy, both of which Hobbes blames for confusing people about the proper relationship between religious and civil authority.

Personal opinion: if reading a 17th-century philosopher score some hits on the Pope is your idea of "fun" reading, then (a) Part IV is definitely for you, and (b) you should check yourself into a mental hospital, because you are not okay.

The Passages Worth Underlining

A few of the passages that stopped us:

On the desire for power (Chapter 11):

So that in the first place, I put for a general inclination of all mankind, a perpetual and restless desire of power after power, that ceaseth only in death.

This is the anthropological premise of the whole book. Hobbes is not saying humans are evil - he is saying humans are driven, and a world of driven people without a common referee is a world at war.

On the state of nature (Chapter 13):

In such condition there is no place for industry, because the fruit thereof is uncertain... no arts; no letters; no society; and which is worst of all, continual fear, and danger of violent death; and the life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short.

The most famous sentence in the book. Worth reading in context - the argument is that everything worthwhile in human life (industry, arts, letters, society) requires predictability, and predictability requires an enforcer.

On the social contract (Chapter 17):

This is the generation of that great LEVIATHAN, or rather (to speak more reverently) of that mortal god to which we owe, under the immortal God, our peace and defence.

The image the whole book takes its name from. The famous frontispiece of the 1651 edition shows a giant crowned king whose body is made of hundreds of tiny individual people - the sovereign as the literal composite of all his subjects.

On liberty (Chapter 21):

The liberty of a subject lieth therefore only in those things which, in regulating their actions, the sovereign hath praetermitted [omitted]: such as is the liberty to buy and sell and otherwise contract with one another; to choose their own abode, their own diet, their own trade of life, and institute their children as they themselves think fit; and the like.

Freedom is whatever the sovereign has not made a law about. This is the definition of liberty that makes modern readers most uncomfortable, and probably the one that is most philosophically distant from what we mean by liberty today.

The One Exception: Self-Preservation

Hobbes "gives" the sovereign nearly unlimited power. But there is one right the sovereign cannot take away, because it is the entire reason people submit to the sovereign in the first place - the right of self-preservation.

From Chapter 21:

If the sovereign command a man (though justly condemned) to kill, wound, or maim himself; or not to resist those that assault him; or to abstain from the use of food, air, medicine, or any other thing without which he cannot live, yet hath that man the liberty to disobey.

This is the entire modern human-rights tradition in embryo. It is also a very narrow exception. You can refuse to kill yourself. You cannot refuse to be taxed, censored, drafted, or otherwise regulated.

Why Read This In 2025

Reading Hobbes today is uncomfortable because his framing is almost the exact opposite of modern political intuition.

We tend to worry about state overreach - the risk that the sovereign will have too much power. Hobbes worried about state collapse - the risk that the sovereign will have too little.

In a country that has been at war with itself, Hobbes's worry is the more urgent one.

The reason to read the book anyway is that it clarifies what liberal democracy is for. Liberal democracy is a very particular set of institutions that try to prevent both failure modes - both state overreach and state collapse - by dividing sovereignty across branches, making the sovereign accountable to elections, and constraining the sovereign with a bill of rights. When you understand Hobbes, you understand what all those institutional constraints are working against. They are working against the pull toward either total sovereignty or none.

You can disagree with Hobbes's conclusions (we do, almost universally) and still find his framing of the problem useful. Political institutions have to solve the problem Hobbes identified: how do we get out of the state of nature without ending up with a tyrant? The modern answer is elaborate and multi-layered, and it mostly works. But it is worth remembering that the alternative Hobbes was writing about - a country tearing itself apart - is not a thought experiment. It happens, in living memory of some readers of this blog, in several countries.

Forever Blue: The Other Side of the Bums Story

Posted Thursday 05/01/2025 in Reading

permalink

We wrote about Peter Golenbock's Bums back in November 2022. That book is an oral history of the Brooklyn Dodgers, and one of its central characters is Walter O'Malley, the owner who moved the team to Los Angeles after the 1957 season. In Golenbock's telling, O'Malley is the villain of the story - the guy who ripped the heart out of Brooklyn for money.

Michael D'Antonio's Forever Blue: The True Story of Walter O'Malley argues that story is incomplete, and in important ways just wrong. We read it in May 2025 and it changed our mind about several things. Full notes on our wiki: Forever Blue.

The Golenbock Version

The short version of the Bums narrative on O'Malley:

  • O'Malley was a lawyer, not a baseball man - he came into the organization representing the Brooklyn Trust Company, which held the mortgage on Ebbets Field
  • He maneuvered Branch Rickey out of the ownership structure in the early 1950s
  • He decided he wanted a new stadium, didn't get exactly the deal he wanted from New York, and packed up and left for Los Angeles
  • The move destroyed a piece of Brooklyn that has never come back

This is the version most Brooklynites believe. It is also the version that appears in almost every English-language baseball history book we have read. The move is treated as a betrayal, and O'Malley as the man who made it.

The D'Antonio Version

D'Antonio had access to O'Malley's personal papers, which is unusual - most O'Malley biographies rely on public records and the accounts of people who hated him. What he found argues for a different framing.

Ebbets Field was genuinely broken. By the mid-1950s, the ballpark was over 40 years old, seated only about 32,000 people, had almost no parking, and sat in a neighborhood where the demographics of the Dodgers' fanbase were rapidly leaving. The postwar suburban migration meant that fans who had walked to Ebbets Field in the 1930s were now driving in from Long Island, and there was nowhere to put the cars. Television revenue was starting to matter, and Ebbets Field was hard to broadcast from. The problem O'Malley was trying to solve was real.

He tried very hard to stay in Brooklyn. For years - the majority of the 1950s - O'Malley was pushing for a new, privately financed stadium in Brooklyn. His preferred site was the intersection of Atlantic and Flatbush Avenues, which is where the Barclays Center sits today. He wanted a domed stadium, which would have been the first domed stadium in the world if he had gotten to build it. He was not asking the city for money. He was asking the city to help him assemble the land, which required using the Title I condemnation mechanism that Robert Moses controlled.

Robert Moses said no. Repeatedly. Moses was the parks commissioner and the most powerful unelected official in New York. He controlled Title I. He would not use it for O'Malley's Atlantic and Flatbush site. What Moses offered instead was Flushing Meadows in Queens - the future site of Shea Stadium, home of the Mets. O'Malley did not want to be in Queens. He wanted to be in Brooklyn.

The negotiations dragged on. The Los Angeles Coliseum group started courting O'Malley, hard. The math for LA was overwhelming: enormous market, no existing team, willing to help with land, huge television territory. And still O'Malley tried to get the Brooklyn deal.

Eventually he ran out of patience and moved.

Robert Moses

The person who comes out worst in D'Antonio's telling is Robert Moses. If you have read Robert Caro's The Power Broker, this will not be surprising - Moses shaped and reshaped the physical form of New York City for decades, and a lot of his decisions were driven by personal aesthetics and personal vendettas rather than any coherent civic vision.

Moses had specific ideas about where major sports facilities belonged (in Flushing Meadows, near his beloved World's Fair site) and no interest in letting O'Malley build somewhere else. He had the power to block the Brooklyn deal, and he used it.

If Moses had said yes to Atlantic and Flatbush in 1955, the Brooklyn Dodgers might still be in Brooklyn. That is not a speculative claim - it is straightforwardly what the sequence of events shows.

What Changed Our Mind

Reading Bums in 2022, we came away with the standard Brooklyn grievance. O'Malley did it. He is the villain.

Reading Forever Blue in 2025, we came away with something messier. O'Malley made the final decision, yes, and he benefited enormously from moving to LA. But the choice was between "move to LA" and "keep losing money in a decaying ballpark I cannot replace because the city won't let me." Given those two options, the LA move looks less like villainy and more like the outcome of a specific political failure - a failure that Robert Moses owns as much as O'Malley does.

The historical villains of Brooklyn baseball, in something like descending order, are probably:

  1. Robert Moses, for using his power to block the deal that would have kept the team
  2. The New York political establishment, for letting Moses hold that much unaccountable power in the first place
  3. Walter O'Malley, for taking the LA offer once Brooklyn was effectively closed
  4. Horace Stoneham (owner of the New York Giants), whose move to San Francisco was more purely opportunistic and gets less attention because the Giants' fanbase never had the emotional grip on Manhattan that the Dodgers had on Brooklyn

D'Antonio is not making O'Malley a hero. He is making him a businessman in an impossible situation, which is a much more interesting portrait than either "villain" or "hero."

Two Books, One Story

The reason to read both books is that they are answering different questions. Golenbock is asking: what did it feel like to lose the Dodgers? D'Antonio is asking: how did that actually happen? Both questions have real answers, and neither answer is complete without the other.

If you can only read one, read Golenbock - the oral history format does something no biography can, which is put you in the room with the fans who lived it. But if you cared enough about that book to still be thinking about it three years later, Forever Blue is what you read next. It's the book that turns the tragedy into a particular kind of history: a decision that could have gone the other way, and didn't, because one specific person with too much power made it not go the other way.

References

  • Our wiki notes: Forever Blue
  • D'Antonio, Michael. Forever Blue: The True Story of Walter O'Malley, Baseball's Most Controversial Owner, and the Dodgers of Brooklyn and Los Angeles. Riverhead Books, 2009.
  • Our earlier take: Reading Bums
  • Robert Moses background: Caro, Robert A. The Power Broker. Alfred A. Knopf, 1974.

Project Euler 312: Hamiltonian Cycles on Sierpiński Graphs

Posted Sunday 04/20/2025 in Mathematics

permalink

Project Euler problem 312 is one of the harder problems we have worked through. It combines graph theory, fractal geometry, combinatorial counting, and modular arithmetic, and the final answer requires computing C(C(C(10000))) mod 13^8 where C(n) is the number of Hamiltonian cycles on a Sierpiński graph of order n.

We ended up leaning on OEIS to get the recurrence in closed form, which we will confess to below. Full notes: Project Euler/312.

The Problem

A Sierpiński graph of order 1, denoted S_1, is an equilateral triangle - three vertices, three edges.

S_{n+1} is built from three copies of S_n positioned so each pair of copies shares one corner vertex.

Let C(n) be the number of cycles that pass through every vertex of S_n exactly once - the number of Hamiltonian cycles. The problem tells us C(1) = C(2) = 1, and C(5) = 71,328,803,586,048.

The goal: find C(C(C(10000))) mod 13^8.

Two things to notice about the goal. First, C(10000) is astronomically large - you cannot actually compute it and then feed it to C again. Second, mod 13^8 is a big hint: the whole calculation is going to happen in modular arithmetic, and there is going to be some periodicity trick.

Vertices and Edges

Some easy facts to nail down before doing anything clever.

Number of edges of S_n:

$$ e(S_n) = 3^{n+1} $$

Number of vertices of S_n:

$$ v(S_n) = \frac{3}{2}\left(3^n + 1\right) $$

So S_10000 has on the order of 3^10000 vertices, which is a number with about 4,700 decimal digits. A Hamiltonian cycle visits every vertex, so the number of Hamiltonian cycles is going to have on the order of 3^10000 factorial-ish in its combinatorics. There is no approach that involves listing anything.

The Recursive Structure

The critical observation is that S_{n+1} is built from three copies of S_n. A Hamiltonian cycle on S_{n+1} has to traverse all three copies, and the only places it can move between copies are at the shared corner vertices. So any Hamiltonian cycle on S_{n+1} consists of:

  • A Hamiltonian path through the first copy of S_n, entering and exiting at two of its three outer corners
  • A Hamiltonian path through the second copy, similarly
  • A Hamiltonian path through the third copy, similarly
  • Plus the three edges that connect the copies together at the shared corners

So counting Hamiltonian cycles reduces to counting certain kinds of Hamiltonian paths on smaller graphs.

Let

  • C(n) = number of Hamiltonian cycles on S_n
  • P(n) = number of Hamiltonian paths on S_n where both endpoints are outer corners
  • P̄(n) = number of Hamiltonian paths on S_n where exactly one endpoint is an outer corner

Then

$$ C(n) = [P(n-1)]^3 $$

(three copies, each contributing a Hamiltonian path with both endpoints at outer corners, one path per copy, chosen independently).

Similarly, by tracking which paths piece together into which combined paths, we get

$$ P(n) = 2 [P(n-1)]^2 P̄(n-1) $$
$$ P̄(n) = 2 [P̄(n-1)]^2 P(n-1) $$

These recurrences let us compute P(n), P̄(n), and C(n) in principle. The values grow explosively - by the time n = 5, C(n) is already 7.1 × 10^13.

Closed Form (Confession Time)

At this point we did the thing everyone does: computed C(1) through C(5) from the recurrence, dropped the sequence into OEIS, and immediately hit A246959 - the "number of Hamiltonian cycles in the Sierpiński graph."

The closed form given by OEIS is

$$ C(n) = 8 \cdot 12^{(3^{n-2} - 3)/2} \qquad n \geq 3 $$

which is very compact. Equivalently, the recurrence

$$ C(n) = (3 \cdot C(n-1))^3 \qquad n \geq 4 $$

Sanity check against the given value C(5) = 71,328,803,586,048. Wolfram Alpha confirms.

First few values:

n C(n)
1 1
2 2
3 8
4 13,824
5 71,328,803,586,048

Note that C(2) = 2 above, but the problem states C(1) = C(2) = 1. The mismatch is a convention issue about what counts as a Hamiltonian cycle on the tiniest graphs (traversal direction, effectively). We used the OEIS convention for anything n ≥ 3, which is where the counting matters.

Nested Evaluation Under a Modulus

Now for the actual goal: C(C(C(10000))) mod 13^8.

The magnitude of C(10000) is not something you can hold in memory. By n = 20 the exponent in 12^((3^(n-2) - 3)/2) is already millions of digits long, and we are being asked about n = 10000. So the whole calculation has to happen without ever materializing an intermediate value in full.

The trick is that we don't need the intermediate values in full. We need C(C(C(10000))) mod 13^8. That means for the outermost C, we only need its argument modulo something that makes the outermost C computable. And that "something" is much smaller than the argument itself.

Three techniques do the work.

Technique 1: Periodicity of C(n) mod M

The sequence C(1), C(2), C(3), ... taken modulo any fixed M is eventually periodic. It has to be - the state at step n in the recurrence C(n) = (3 · C(n-1))^3 mod M is a single residue in Z/MZ, and there are only M possible residues, so the sequence must eventually enter a cycle.

Let P be the period. Then

$$ C(X) \bmod M = C(((X - X_0) \bmod P) + X_0) \bmod M $$

for X at least as large as the pre-period offset X_0. In practice you compute successive C(n) mod M values until you see a repeat, and that tells you X_0 and P.

This is the workhorse. It lets you reduce "compute C at an astronomically large index" to "compute C at an index in the range [X_0, X_0 + P)."

Technique 2: Choosing the Right Modulus at Each Layer

The three C calls need different moduli.

The outermost C operates modulo 13^8. That is what the problem asks for.

The middle C needs to produce a residue that determines the outermost C(x) mod 13^8. By technique 1, the outermost C is periodic mod 13^8 with some period P_out. So the middle C only needs to compute its result modulo P_out (well, modulo the pre-period plus period - be careful with the offset).

The innermost C(10000) needs to produce a residue that determines the middle C(y) mod P_out. So find the period P_mid of C mod P_out, and compute C(10000) mod P_mid.

So the algorithm is:

  1. Find the period of C(n) mod 13^8. Call it P_out.
  2. Find the period of C(n) mod P_out. Call it P_mid.
  3. Compute C(10000) mod P_mid. Call the result a.
  4. Compute C(a) mod P_out. Call the result b.
  5. Compute C(b) mod 13^8.

Each step uses either the recurrence directly (b = (3·b_prev)^3 mod M) or, for the innermost step at n = 10000, the recurrence run 10000 times mod the appropriate modulus. Ten thousand modular multiplications is nothing.

Technique 3: Handling the Cube Under a Prime Power

The recurrence C(n) = (3 · C(n-1))^3 involves a cube, so under a prime power modulus you have to be careful about invertibility. Cubing is well-defined mod 13^8 (or mod anything else), so the forward recurrence is not a problem - you just cube and multiply. What can go wrong is if you try to invert the cubing at some point, which you would need if you tried to do matrix exponentiation on the logarithm of the recurrence.

We did not need to invert. The three-layer periodicity approach above uses only forward evaluation of the recurrence, so cubing mod 13^8 is fine.

Chinese Remainder Theorem

CRT is worth mentioning for completeness. If the modulus had been composite with multiple distinct prime factors, we would compute the answer separately modulo each prime power and glue the results together with CRT.

Here the modulus is 13^8, a single prime power, so CRT is not needed - but if the problem had asked for mod (7^5 · 11^3 · 13^8), the approach would be to solve three separate problems and CRT the answers.

What We Learned

Three things worth carrying forward.

Recursive structure beats brute force by a lot. The naive approach of enumerating cycles on S_n is impossible for anything n > 4 or so. The recursive approach reduces the problem to counting paths on S_{n-1} with three specific endpoint types, and that recursion collapses to a single-variable formula. Every time you can find a recursive structure, use it.

OEIS is not cheating. Recognizing a sequence and looking up its closed form is a legitimate research tool, not shortcut. The people who computed and cataloged OEIS A246959 did the work of proving the closed form. Standing on their shoulders is what mathematics has always looked like. The alternative - re-deriving every known result from scratch - is how you never finish anything.

Nested modular evaluation is a real technique. When a problem asks for f(f(f(n))) mod M with a huge n, you rarely need to compute the intermediate values. You work outward-in through periods: the outermost period tells you what modulus the middle layer actually needs to produce, which tells you what modulus the innermost layer needs to produce. Astronomical intermediate values reduce to residues in tractable rings. This shape of problem shows up a lot in number theory and cryptography, and it is worth having the pattern in your head.
