*Bums: An Oral History of the Brooklyn Dodgers* by Peter Golenbock is
exactly what it says on the tin. It is an oral history, which means the
book is stitched together mostly out of firsthand accounts - players,
managers, executives, sportswriters, and fans - with Golenbock providing
just enough connective tissue to keep the timeline moving.

We read it in the fall of 2022 and it turned out to be one of the better
baseball books we have picked up. Below are our notes, chapter by chapter.

## The Setup

The Brooklyn Dodgers were, until 1957, one of the two National League
teams in New York (the other being the Giants), and the Dodgers are one
of the great tragic love stories in American sports. They lost, and lost,
and lost, and then finally won a championship in 1955, and then two years
later Walter O'Malley moved the team to Los Angeles and the borough of
Brooklyn never really recovered.

The nickname "Bums" was affectionate. Sort of.

## Chapter-by-Chapter

**Prologue.** Sets the stage. Establishes why Brooklyn and its baseball
team had a relationship that was different in kind from other city-team
relationships. Introduces the oral history format and the "Bums" nickname.

**Chapter 1 - The Daffiness Boys.** Covers the pre-WWII years. This is the
era of Babe Herman getting hit on the head with fly balls, Casey Stengel
managing the team into and out of trouble, and the general reputation of
the Dodgers as lovably incompetent. Ebbets Field is the third character.

**Chapter 2 - MacPhail.** Larry MacPhail arrives as general manager in the
late 1930s. He is the person who brings night baseball to Ebbets Field,
brings radio broadcasts (with Red Barber calling games), and generally
drags the Dodgers into professional respectability. Team gets competitive.
Wins the 1941 pennant. Loses the World Series to the Yankees, which is
the beginning of a pattern.

**Chapter 3 - Rickey.** Branch Rickey arrives after MacPhail leaves for
war service (and then, later, jumps to the Yankees). Rickey builds out a
minor-league farm system, which was a serious strategic innovation at the
time. The chapter sets up the next one.

**Chapter 4 - The Noble Experiment.** The Jackie Robinson chapter. It is
the emotional center of the book. Golenbock gets voices from Robinson
himself (via earlier interviews), teammates like Pee Wee Reese, opponents,
Rickey, and observers. The abuse Robinson faced is described directly, not
softened. His talent and his composure both come through. This chapter is
worth the price of the book on its own.

**Chapter 5 - The Boys of Summer.** The core roster that would define the
1940s and 1950s Dodgers: Duke Snider, Gil Hodges, Roy Campanella, Carl
Furillo, Carl Erskine, Preacher Roe, Pee Wee Reese, Billy Cox. The
chapter is mostly personalities and clubhouse chemistry, and it is a lot
of fun.

**Chapter 6 - Wait 'Til Next Year.** The heartbreak chapter. Repeated
losses to the Yankees in the World Series. The 1951 playoff loss to the
Giants on Bobby Thomson's "Shot Heard 'Round the World." The rivalry with
the Giants, which was intense in a way that modern baseball rivalries
mostly are not. And the perennial fan slogan that names the chapter.

**Chapter 7 - Nineteen Fifty-Five.** The one year they won it all. After
all the near-misses, the Dodgers beat the Yankees in the 1955 World Series.
Johnny Podres's Game 7 shutout gets the spotlight. The chapter captures a
level of borough-wide joy that is very hard to imagine now.

**Chapter 8 - The End.** The move. Walter O'Malley consolidates power
after Rickey leaves, decides he wants a new stadium, runs into Robert
Moses in the process, and eventually decides Los Angeles is a better bet
than Brooklyn. Players, sportswriters, and fans express shock, betrayal,
and grief in that order. The 1957 season is the last one in Brooklyn.

**Epilogue.** Reflections on what the team meant, what the borough lost,
and what the departure symbolized about postwar American cities in
general.

## What Makes the Book Good

The oral history format works because so many of the primary sources were
still alive when Golenbock did the interviews. You get Pee Wee Reese
talking about Jackie Robinson in his own voice. You get Ebbets Field vendors
and ticket-takers remembering what it smelled like. You get sportswriters
talking about deadlines and games they covered from the press box.

If you want a synthesized, analytical baseball history, this is not that
book. Golenbock's editorial hand is light. He is stitching, not arguing.

If you want to hear what it was like to be there, this is that book.

## Recommended If

* You like baseball history.
* You like New York history.
* You have ever wondered why Brooklyn is the way Brooklyn is.
* You want a Jackie Robinson chapter that treats him as a person and an
  athlete instead of an icon.
